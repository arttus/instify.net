# Legal Practice Areas - Common Client Questions

## Corporate and Business Law

### Business Formation

**Q: What type of business entity should I form?**

The choice depends on several factors including liability protection, tax treatment, management structure, and future goals. Common options include:

- **Sole Proprietorship**: Simplest structure, no legal separation between owner and business, unlimited personal liability
- **LLC (Limited Liability Company)**: Protects personal assets, flexible tax treatment, less formality than corporation
- **S Corporation**: Pass-through taxation, protects personal assets, limited to 100 shareholders, must be U.S. citizens/residents
- **C Corporation**: Separate tax entity, unlimited shareholders, easier to raise capital, double taxation on dividends
- **Partnership**: Multiple owners, shared management and profits, general partners have unlimited liability
- **Professional Corporation/LLC**: For licensed professionals (doctors, lawyers, accountants)

Factors to consider: number of owners, liability exposure, tax implications, fundraising plans, exit strategy, administrative burden.

**Q: How long does it take to form a business entity?**

Timeline varies by state and entity type:
- LLC or Corporation filing: 1-2 weeks after submitting articles of organization/incorporation
- Expedited filing: 24-48 hours (additional fee)
- EIN from IRS: Immediate online or 4-6 weeks by mail
- Operating agreement/bylaws: 1-2 weeks to draft
- Complete formation package: Typically 2-4 weeks total

**Q: What documents do I need to start a business?**

Essential formation documents:
- Articles of Organization/Incorporation filed with state
- Operating Agreement (LLC) or Bylaws (Corporation)
- Federal EIN from IRS
- State tax registration
- Business licenses and permits (industry-specific)
- Initial meeting minutes and resolutions
- Stock certificates or membership certificates
- Registered agent designation

### Contract Law

**Q: Do I need a written contract or is a verbal agreement enough?**

While some verbal contracts are legally enforceable, written contracts are strongly recommended because:
- They provide clear evidence of agreement terms
- They prevent misunderstandings and disputes
- Certain contracts must be in writing by law (Statute of Frauds): real estate sales, contracts over $500, agreements lasting over one year, promises to pay someone else's debt
- Written contracts are easier to enforce in court
- They demonstrate professionalism and protect both parties

**Q: What makes a contract legally binding?**

Essential elements of a valid contract:
1. **Offer**: Clear proposal with specific terms
2. **Acceptance**: Unambiguous agreement to the offer
3. **Consideration**: Exchange of value (money, services, promises)
4. **Mutual Intent**: Both parties intend to be bound
5. **Capacity**: Parties are legally able to contract (age, mental competence)
6. **Legality**: Purpose is lawful

Additional factors strengthening enforceability: written documentation, signatures, witnesses, notarization (when required).

**Q: How do I get out of a contract?**

Options for exiting a contract:
- **Mutual agreement**: Both parties agree to terminate
- **Breach by other party**: Material breach may allow termination
- **Contract terms**: Check for termination clauses, notice requirements
- **Impossibility**: Performance becomes impossible through no fault
- **Frustration of purpose**: Contract purpose is defeated by unforeseen event
- **Rescission**: Contract voidable due to fraud, duress, mistake, or incapacity
- **Force majeure**: Unforeseeable circumstances prevent performance

Consequences of improper termination: breach of contract damages, specific performance, loss of deposits/payments.

### Employment Law

**Q: Can I be fired without cause in my state?**

Most U.S. employment is "at-will," meaning employers can terminate employment at any time for any lawful reason or no reason. However, exceptions include:

**Protected categories** - Cannot fire based on:
- Race, color, national origin, religion, sex (Title VII)
- Age over 40 (ADEA)
- Disability (ADA)
- Pregnancy (PDA)
- Genetic information (GINA)
- Military service (USERRA)
- State-specific protected classes (sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status, etc.)

**Other protections**:
- Retaliation for whistleblowing or protected complaints
- Taking protected leave (FMLA, workers' comp)
- Union activities or collective bargaining
- Refusing illegal activities
- Public policy violations
- Employment contract provisions
- Implied contracts through employee handbooks
- Covenant of good faith and fair dealing (some states)

**Q: What should I do if I'm experiencing workplace harassment or discrimination?**

Recommended steps:
1. **Document everything**: Keep detailed records of incidents (dates, times, witnesses, what was said/done)
2. **Review company policy**: Check employee handbook for reporting procedures
3. **Report internally**: File complaint with HR or designated person following company procedures
4. **Preserve evidence**: Save emails, texts, photos, performance reviews
5. **File external complaint**: EEOC (federal) or state civil rights agency (within 180-300 days of incident)
6. **Consult attorney**: Get legal advice on your rights and options
7. **Avoid retaliation**: Document any adverse actions after complaint

Don't quit without consulting an attorney - resignation may affect legal claims.

**Q: Am I entitled to severance pay?**

Severance pay is not legally required unless:
- Employment contract guarantees it
- Company policy promises it
- Union agreement requires it
- Part of mass layoff/plant closing under WARN Act
- State-specific laws mandate it

Employers often offer severance in exchange for release of legal claims. Before signing:
- Review carefully - releases are generally binding
- Negotiate terms if possible
- Consider consulting attorney, especially if discrimination or wrongful termination suspected
- You typically have 21 days to review (45 days if group termination) and 7 days to revoke after signing
- Don't sign under pressure

### Intellectual Property

**Q: Do I need to trademark my business name?**

Trademark registration provides important benefits:

**Advantages**:
- Nationwide exclusive rights to use mark
- Legal presumption of ownership
- Public notice of claim
- Ability to use ® symbol
- Basis for international registration
- Can sue in federal court
- Enhanced damages for infringement
- Prevents similar trademark registration

**When to register**:
- Planning significant marketing investment
- Expanding beyond local area
- E-commerce or nationwide business
- High risk of infringement in your industry
- Building brand value and recognition

**Alternatives**:
- Common law rights through use (limited to geographic area)
- State trademark registration (protection within state)

Cost: $250-$350 per class of goods/services (federal filing fee) plus attorney fees.

**Q: How do I protect my business idea?**

Ideas alone cannot be protected, but implementations can:

**Patents**: Protect inventions, processes, designs
- Utility patents: New and useful processes, machines, compositions
- Design patents: Ornamental designs
- Requirements: Novel, non-obvious, useful
- Duration: 20 years (utility), 15 years (design)
- Cost: $10,000-$15,000+ including attorney fees

**Trade Secrets**: Protect confidential business information
- Customer lists, formulas, processes, methods
- Must take reasonable steps to maintain secrecy
- Non-disclosure agreements (NDAs)
- Employee confidentiality agreements
- Potentially unlimited duration

**Copyrights**: Protect original creative works
- Software code, websites, marketing materials, content
- Automatic upon creation, registration enhances rights
- Duration: Life of author plus 70 years

**Trademarks**: Protect brand identity
- Names, logos, slogans, trade dress

**Business strategies**:
- First-to-market advantage
- NDAs with partners and employees
- Non-compete agreements (where enforceable)
- Documentation and proper agreements

## Litigation and Dispute Resolution

### Civil Litigation

**Q: Should I sue or try to settle out of court?**

Factors to consider:

**Settlement advantages**:
- Faster resolution (months vs. years)
- Lower costs (avoid trial expenses)
- Predictable outcome
- Privacy (no public record)
- Preserves relationships
- Less stressful
- Both parties have input

**Litigation advantages**:
- May recover more money
- Public vindication
- Sets precedent
- Court enforces judgment
- Discovery process reveals evidence
- Appeals available

**Decision factors**:
- Strength of your case
- Amount in dispute vs. litigation costs
- Time sensitivity
- Evidence available
- Willingness of other party to negotiate
- Importance of public record
- Emotional vs. practical considerations

Most cases settle before trial - litigation can pressure settlement.

**Q: How long does a lawsuit take?**

Timeline varies significantly based on:

**Typical timeline**:
- Filing to answer: 20-30 days
- Discovery phase: 6-12 months
- Motions and hearings: 3-6 months
- Trial preparation: 3-6 months
- Trial: Days to weeks
- Total: 1-3 years to trial for standard case
- Appeals add 1-2+ years

**Factors affecting duration**:
- Court backlog and scheduling
- Case complexity
- Number of parties
- Discovery disputes
- Motion practice
- Settlement negotiations
- Attorney availability
- Jurisdiction (federal vs. state, specific court)

Simple cases may resolve in 6-12 months. Complex commercial litigation can take 3-5+ years.

**Q: What are my alternatives to going to court?**

Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) options:

**Mediation**:
- Neutral third party facilitates negotiation
- Non-binding (unless settlement reached)
- Informal process
- Parties control outcome
- Typically 1-2 sessions
- Cost: $200-$500/hour for mediator split between parties
- Success rate: 70-80%

**Arbitration**:
- Private judge hears case and renders decision
- Usually binding
- Less formal than court
- Faster than litigation
- Limited discovery
- Limited appeals
- Cost varies: filing fees plus arbitrator fees
- Common in contracts (mandatory arbitration clauses)

**Negotiation**:
- Direct discussion between parties or attorneys
- Informal, flexible
- No third party involvement
- Settlement agreements are binding

**Other options**:
- Collaborative law (team approach)
- Neutral evaluation
- Summary jury trial
- Mini-trial

Many contracts require ADR before litigation.

### Personal Injury

**Q: How much is my personal injury case worth?**

Case value depends on multiple factors:

**Economic damages** (quantifiable):
- Past and future medical expenses
- Lost wages and earning capacity
- Property damage
- Out-of-pocket costs
- Rehabilitation and therapy

**Non-economic damages** (subjective):
- Pain and suffering
- Emotional distress
- Loss of enjoyment of life
- Disfigurement or scarring
- Loss of consortium (spouse)

**Factors affecting value**:
- Severity of injuries
- Permanence of disability
- Clear liability
- Available insurance coverage
- Quality of medical documentation
- Plaintiff's credibility and background
- Jurisdiction and venue
- Defendant's assets

**Punitive damages** (rare):
- Only when defendant's conduct was egregious
- Meant to punish and deter
- Varies greatly by state

Average settlements vary widely from thousands to millions depending on injury severity.

**Q: How long do I have to file a personal injury lawsuit?**

Statute of limitations varies by state and claim type:

**Typical time limits**:
- Personal injury: 1-6 years (commonly 2-3 years)
- Medical malpractice: 1-3 years
- Product liability: 2-4 years
- Wrongful death: 1-3 years
- Property damage: 2-6 years
- Professional malpractice: 1-4 years

**Clock typically starts**:
- Date of injury or accident
- Discovery of injury (discovery rule)
- Minor's 18th birthday (for childhood injuries)
- Date of death (wrongful death)

**Important exceptions**:
- Government claims: Often 6 months to file notice
- Minors: Clock may not start until age 18
- Fraud or concealment: May extend deadline
- Continuing treatment: May toll statute

Missing deadline usually means permanent loss of right to sue. Consult attorney promptly.

## Real Estate Law

### Residential Real Estate

**Q: Do I need a lawyer to buy or sell a house?**

Legal requirements vary by state:

**States requiring attorney**:
Some states require or strongly encourage attorney involvement in residential transactions.

**When attorney is recommended**:
- Complex transactions or unusual terms
- For-sale-by-owner (FSBO) deals
- Short sales or foreclosures
- Properties with title issues
- Estate sales or trust properties
- Investment properties
- New construction
- Disputes or concerns

**What real estate attorney does**:
- Review and negotiate purchase agreement
- Examine title and resolve issues
- Review title insurance policy
- Explain contract terms and implications
- Attend closing and review documents
- Ensure proper deed recording
- Handle escrow and wire transfers
- Resolve last-minute problems

**Cost**: Typically $500-$2,000 flat fee

Even if not required, attorney review can prevent costly mistakes.

**Q: What happens if I can't make my mortgage payments?**

Options before foreclosure:

**Work with lender**:
- **Forbearance**: Temporary pause or reduction
- **Modification**: Permanent loan term changes
- **Repayment plan**: Catch up over time
- **Refinance**: New loan with better terms (if you qualify)

**Alternatives to foreclosure**:
- **Short sale**: Sell for less than owed (lender approval required)
- **Deed in lieu**: Transfer property to lender
- **Loan assumption**: Transfer to qualified buyer

**Government programs**:
- FHA/VA/USDA assistance programs
- State and local foreclosure prevention
- Housing counseling agencies (HUD-approved)

**Bankruptcy**:
- Chapter 13: Stop foreclosure, repay arrears over 3-5 years
- Chapter 7: Temporary automatic stay

**What not to do**:
- Ignore lender communications
- Abandon the property
- Stop paying without plan
- Fall for foreclosure rescue scams

**Timeline matters**: Act quickly - more options available before foreclosure filing.

### Commercial Real Estate

**Q: What should I know before signing a commercial lease?**

Key lease terms to negotiate and understand:

**Financial terms**:
- Base rent and escalation clauses
- Additional rent (CAM, taxes, insurance)
- Security deposit amount
- Personal guarantee requirements
- Rent abatement or free rent periods

**Lease structure**:
- **Gross lease**: Landlord pays most expenses
- **Net lease**: Tenant pays some/all operating costs
  - Single net (N): Tenant pays property taxes
  - Double net (NN): Taxes + insurance
  - Triple net (NNN): Taxes + insurance + maintenance
- **Modified gross**: Negotiated split of expenses

**Important clauses**:
- Permitted use restrictions
- Exclusivity provisions
- Assignment and subletting rights
- Renewal and expansion options
- Early termination provisions
- Maintenance and repair responsibilities
- Leasehold improvement terms
- Default and remedies
- Parking allocation
- Signage rights

**Review carefully**:
- Square footage calculation (usable vs. rentable)
- Operating expense pass-throughs and caps
- Landlord's rights to relocate tenant
- Rules and regulations
- Environmental responsibilities
- Insurance requirements

Have attorney review before signing - commercial leases heavily favor landlords.

## Family Law

### Divorce and Separation

**Q: What's the difference between contested and uncontested divorce?**

**Uncontested divorce**:
- Both spouses agree on all issues
- Faster (2-6 months typically)
- Less expensive ($1,500-$5,000 with attorney)
- Less stressful
- Requires agreement on: property division, debt allocation, child custody, child support, spousal support

**Contested divorce**:
- Spouses disagree on one or more issues
- Longer process (1-3+ years)
- More expensive ($15,000-$50,000+ per spouse)
- Involves discovery, motions, possibly trial
- Court decides disputed issues

**Issues commonly contested**:
- Child custody and visitation
- Division of assets and debts
- Spousal support amount and duration
- Business valuation and division
- Retirement account division
- Real estate disposition

Many contested divorces settle before trial. Even one disagreement makes divorce "contested."

**Q: How is property divided in a divorce?**

Division depends on state law:

**Community property states** (9 states):
- Property acquired during marriage divided 50/50
- Separate property (owned before marriage, inherited, gifted) stays with owner
- Debt incurred during marriage also split 50/50

**Equitable distribution states** (41 states):
- Property divided fairly, not necessarily equally
- Court considers multiple factors:
  - Length of marriage
  - Each spouse's income and earning potential
  - Contributions to marriage (financial and non-financial)
  - Age and health
  - Standard of living during marriage
  - Child custody arrangements
  - Separate property amounts
  - Marital misconduct (some states)

**Common division issues**:
- **Family home**: Sell and split, one buys out other, or delayed sale
- **Retirement accounts**: Divided by QDRO
- **Business interests**: Valued and divided or offset with other assets
- **Debt**: Allocated based on who incurred and benefited
- **Professional degrees/licenses**: Some states consider marital property

**Marital vs. separate property**:
- Marital: Acquired during marriage
- Separate: Owned before marriage, inherited, received as gift, personal injury recovery
- Commingling can make separate property marital

**Q: What factors determine child custody?**

Courts prioritize "best interests of the child" considering:

**Primary factors**:
- Child's age, sex, and health
- Emotional bond with each parent
- Each parent's ability to provide care
- Stability of home environment
- Child's adjustment to school and community
- Mental and physical health of parents
- History of domestic violence or abuse
- Substance abuse issues
- Each parent's willingness to facilitate relationship with other parent
- Child's preference (if age-appropriate, typically 12+)
- Siblings staying together
- Work schedules and availability
- Geographic proximity

**Types of custody**:
- **Legal custody**: Decision-making authority (education, healthcare, religion)
- **Physical custody**: Where child lives
- **Sole custody**: One parent has custody
- **Joint custody**: Both parents share custody
- **Primary custody**: Child lives mainly with one parent, other has visitation

**Custody arrangement considerations**:
- Parenting schedule and visitation
- Holiday and vacation time
- Decision-making processes
- Communication requirements
- Right of first refusal
- Relocation restrictions

Most courts prefer joint legal custody with one primary physical custodian or shared physical custody when parents cooperate.

### Child Support

**Q: How is child support calculated?**

Child support calculation varies by state but generally considers:

**Income shares model** (majority of states):
- Combines both parents' income
- Determines support based on income and number of children
- Allocates proportionally based on each parent's income percentage

**Percentage of income model**:
- Non-custodial parent pays percentage of income
- Percentage varies by number of children

**Factors considered**:
- Gross income of both parents
- Number of children
- Custody arrangement and parenting time
- Child's needs (healthcare, education, childcare)
- Standard of living before separation
- Each parent's ability to pay

**Income sources**:
- Wages and salary
- Bonuses and commissions
- Self-employment income
- Investment income
- Rental income
- Unemployment benefits
- Social Security benefits
- Disability payments
- Workers' compensation

**Deductions from income**:
- Other child support obligations
- Alimony/spousal support paid
- Mandatory retirement contributions (limited)

**Additional expenses**:
- Health insurance premiums
- Unreimbursed medical expenses
- Childcare costs
- Educational expenses
- Extracurricular activities

**Deviation factors**:
- Extraordinary medical needs
- Special educational needs
- Significant parenting time by non-custodial parent
- Child's own income/assets
- Parent's extraordinary expenses

Support continues until child reaches age of majority (18-21 depending on state), becomes emancipated, or finishes high school/college (in some states).

## Estate Planning

### Wills and Trusts

**Q: What's the difference between a will and a trust?**

**Will**:
- Legal document directing asset distribution after death
- Takes effect only upon death
- Must go through probate court
- Public record
- Can name guardians for minor children
- Can name executor
- Easier and less expensive to create
- Can be changed anytime while competent

**Trust**:
- Legal arrangement where trustee holds assets for beneficiaries
- Takes effect immediately when created
- Avoids probate for trust assets
- Private (not public record)
- Can provide asset management during life and after death
- More complex and expensive to create
- Can protect assets from creditors (irrevocable trusts)
- Provides control over distribution timing

**Revocable living trust**:
- Most common trust for estate planning
- Creator maintains control during lifetime
- Assets avoid probate
- Provides for incapacity management
- Can be changed or revoked
- No asset protection from creditors
- Requires funding (transferring assets into trust)

**Key differences**:
- Trusts avoid probate; wills don't
- Trusts are private; wills are public
- Trusts provide incapacity planning; wills don't
- Trusts more complex and expensive
- Wills can name guardians; trusts can't

Many people have both: trust for major assets, will as backup ("pour-over will").

**Q: Do I need an estate plan if I don't have much money?**

Estate planning isn't just for wealthy people. Everyone benefits:

**Non-financial reasons**:
- **Healthcare decisions**: Medical power of attorney, living will, HIPAA authorization
- **Guardianship**: Name guardians for minor children
- **End-of-life wishes**: Burial preferences, organ donation
- **Avoid family conflict**: Clear instructions prevent disputes
- **Incapacity planning**: Who manages affairs if you can't

**Financial benefits even with modest assets**:
- **Avoid intestacy**: State law decides who gets assets (may not match your wishes)
- **Simplify administration**: Easier for family
- **Minimize costs**: Proper planning reduces legal expenses
- **Protect beneficiaries**: Special needs trusts, spendthrift provisions
- **Tax planning**: Even modest estates benefit
- **Retirement accounts**: Beneficiary designations need review

**Basic estate plan includes**:
- Last will and testament
- Durable power of attorney (financial)
- Healthcare power of attorney
- Living will/advance directive
- HIPAA authorization
- Beneficiary designations review

**Cost**: Basic estate plan typically $1,000-$3,000

Without estate plan, state law decides everything - often not what you'd choose.

### Powers of Attorney

**Q: What is a power of attorney and why do I need one?**

**Power of Attorney (POA)**: Legal document authorizing someone to act on your behalf

**Types**:

**Financial/Durable Power of Attorney**:
- Manages financial and legal matters
- "Durable" means continues if you become incapacitated
- Agent can: pay bills, manage investments, file taxes, buy/sell property, manage business
- Continues until death or revocation
- Essential for incapacity planning

**Healthcare Power of Attorney** (Healthcare Proxy):
- Makes medical decisions if you can't
- Agent consults with doctors, authorizes treatment, decides care facility
- Separate from living will (which states your wishes)
- Critical for medical emergencies

**Springing POA**:
- Takes effect only upon specific event (usually incapacity)
- Requires proving incapacity (can cause delays)
- Less commonly used now

**Limited/Special POA**:
- Specific purpose only (e.g., close real estate transaction)
- Temporary or task-specific
- Expires when task complete or date specified

**Why you need POA**:
- **Incapacity protection**: Without POA, family needs court-appointed guardianship
- **Convenience**: Someone can handle affairs during illness, travel, military service
- **Cost savings**: Guardianship proceedings expensive ($5,000-$10,000+)
- **Privacy**: Guardianship is public; POA is private
- **Control**: You choose agent; court chooses guardian

**Choosing agent**:
- Trustworthy and financially responsible
- Capable of handling tasks
- Available and willing
- Understands your values and wishes
- Lives nearby (helpful but not required)

POA ends at death - executor/personal representative takes over via will.

## Immigration Law

**Q: What's the difference between a visa and a green card?**

**Visa**:
- Temporary permission to enter/stay in U.S.
- Specific purpose and time limit
- Must maintain status (employment, student enrollment, etc.)
- Types: tourist (B-2), business (B-1), student (F-1), work (H-1B, L-1, etc.)
- Requires renewal or status change
- No path to citizenship by itself

**Green Card** (Permanent Resident Card):
- Permission to live and work permanently in U.S.
- Not time-limited (card renewed every 10 years, status is permanent)
- Can travel freely in/out of U.S.
- Path to citizenship (can apply after 3-5 years)
- Nearly all employment rights
- Access to some government benefits
- Must maintain permanent residence

**Key differences**:
- Green card is permanent; visa is temporary
- Green card holders can work anywhere; visa holders limited
- Green card leads to citizenship; visa doesn't
- Green card more difficult to obtain
- Losing green card requires extended absence or serious violation; visa expires

**Paths to green card**:
- Family sponsorship (spouse, parent, sibling of U.S. citizen/permanent resident)
- Employment sponsorship (job offer from U.S. employer)
- Diversity lottery
- Refugee/asylum status
- Investment (EB-5)

**Q: How can I sponsor a family member for immigration?**

U.S. citizens and permanent residents can sponsor certain family members:

**U.S. Citizens can sponsor**:
- Spouse (no wait)
- Unmarried children under 21 (no wait)
- Unmarried children over 21 (wait time)
- Married children any age (wait time)
- Parents (if sponsor over 21, no wait)
- Brothers and sisters (long wait time)

**Permanent Residents can sponsor**:
- Spouse (2-3 year wait typically)
- Unmarried children (2-7 year wait depending on age)
- Cannot sponsor parents or siblings

**Process**:
1. **Petition**: File Form I-130 (Petition for Alien Relative)
2. **Wait**: Priority date assigned, wait for visa availability
3. **Application**: Beneficiary applies for immigrant visa or adjustment of status
4. **Interview**: Attend visa interview at embassy/consulate or USCIS
5. **Approval**: Receive immigrant visa or green card

**Requirements**:
- Prove family relationship (birth certificates, marriage certificate)
- Sponsor must meet income requirements (125% of poverty level)
- Sponsor signs affidavit of support (financial responsibility)
- Beneficiary must pass medical exam and background check
- No disqualifying criminal or immigration violations

**Wait times**:
- Immediate relatives (spouse, minor children, parents): No quota, minimal wait
- Family preference categories: 2-20+ years depending on category and country

**Costs**:
- USCIS filing fees: $535-$1,760
- Medical exam: $200-$500
- Attorney fees: $2,000-$5,000+

Process complex - attorney recommended for most cases.

## Criminal Law

**Q: What should I do if I'm arrested?**

**Immediate steps**:

1. **Remain silent**: You have right to remain silent - USE IT
   - Don't explain, don't make excuses, don't tell your side
   - Police can use anything you say against you
   - Say: "I'm invoking my right to remain silent"

2. **Request attorney**: Ask for lawyer immediately
   - Say: "I want to speak with an attorney"
   - Police must stop questioning
   - Don't waive this right

3. **Don't consent to searches**: Police need warrant or probable cause
   - Say: "I do not consent to any searches"
   - Don't physically resist, but clearly state non-consent

4. **Don't resist**: Comply with arrest physically
   - Keep hands visible
   - Follow commands
   - Don't argue or become aggressive
   - Resisting adds charges

5. **Document everything**: As soon as possible
   - Write down what happened
   - Get witness names and contact info
   - Note police badge numbers
   - Photograph any injuries

6. **Contact attorney**: Call as soon as permitted
   - Use your phone call for attorney, not family
   - Public defender if you can't afford attorney
   - Don't discuss case with anyone except attorney

**What NOT to do**:
- Don't talk to police without attorney (even to "explain")
- Don't sign anything without attorney review
- Don't believe promises like "talk and go home"
- Don't discuss case with cellmates or on recorded jail phones
- Don't post on social media
- Don't try to hide or destroy evidence

**After arrest**:
- Arraignment (first court appearance) within 24-48 hours
- Bail determination
- Enter not guilty plea
- Begin working with attorney on defense

Even if innocent, get attorney immediately. Anything you say can hurt your case.

**Q: What's the difference between a misdemeanor and a felony?**

**Misdemeanor**:
- Less serious crimes
- Maximum penalty typically 1 year in county jail
- Fines up to $1,000-$5,000
- Examples: DUI (first offense), petty theft, simple assault, disorderly conduct, trespassing
- Classes A/B/C (or 1/2/3) by severity
- Tried in lower courts
- Record can often be expunged

**Felony**:
- Serious crimes
- Penalty of 1+ years in state prison
- Substantial fines (often $10,000+)
- Examples: murder, rape, robbery, burglary, grand theft, drug trafficking, assault with deadly weapon
- Classes: capital, first degree, second degree, third degree (varies by state)
- Tried in superior/district courts
- Permanent criminal record
- Loss of rights (voting, gun ownership, professional licenses)

**Consequences comparison**:

Misdemeanors:
- Shorter jail time or probation
- Lesser fines
- Easier to expunge
- Less employment impact
- Keep most rights

Felonies:
- Lengthy prison sentences
- Steep fines
- Difficult to expunge
- Significant employment barriers
- Professional license revocation
- Loss of voting rights (varies by state)
- Can't own firearms
- Immigration consequences for non-citizens
- Housing discrimination
- Federal benefits ineligible

**Wobblers**: Some crimes can be charged as either misdemeanor or felony depending on circumstances and criminal history (e.g., theft, assault, drug possession).

Felony conviction has lifelong consequences - strong defense essential.
